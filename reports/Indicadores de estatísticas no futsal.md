# Futsal stats run far deeper than goals

Professional and semi-professional futsal analysis, once you move past the scoreboard, converges on roughly **nine indicator families** — finishing, passing/possession, phase-of-game and transition, defensive actions, numerical-situation play, set pieces, discipline, and two distinct goalkeeper domains (shot-stopping and the flying-goalkeeper/goleiro-linha tactic) — and each indicator within them is tracked at one of three granularities: a simple **tally** (count only), a **tally with outcome** (success/fail baked into the action name or a separate result field), or a **tally with rich detail** (zone, technique/surface, numerical context, or triggering cause attached to the event). Roughly two-thirds of what gets coded in the academic literature is generic to any invasion sport — shots, passes, tackles, interceptions, corners, free kicks, cards — but futsal layers on a genuinely unique sublayer: the **accumulated-team-fouls rule** that converts the 6th-plus foul into a direct free kick with no defensive wall from the second penalty spot ([FIFA Inside](https://inside.fifa.com/news/the-rules-of-futsal-explained)); the **goleiro-linha/linha-goleiro** pairing, where academic studies track the goalkeeper-as-attacker and the outfield-player-as-goalkeeper as two *separately coded* tactics rather than one ([Dialnet 6816971](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/6816971.pdf)); and a **four-moments/four-lines** phase framework that has no clean 11-a-side analogue. No single commercial platform or federation publishes one authoritative "master list" — Nacsport, Hudl Sportscode, and LongoMatch are all blank-canvas button builders with no documented futsal template, and even FIFA/UEFA technical reports stay narrative rather than publishing a standardized GK scorecard — so the taxonomy below is a synthesis built from academic notational studies, rule documents, and the one dedicated free futsal tagging tool found (sportslabtools.com) that does publish its category list.

## Shooting and finishing indicators cluster on zone, type, and surface — not just on/off target

The baseline finishing taxonomy (goal, shot on target, shot off target, shot blocked/intercepted) is generic to any invasion sport and shows up in essentially every study screened, including a 2024 descriptive study of the AFC Women's Futsal Championship that tracked seven attacking indicators (goals, assists, total shots, shots on target, shooting accuracy, shots off target, blocked shots) across 26 matches and found goals correlated strongly with assists, total shots, and shots on target, but **not** with shooting accuracy ([UPI/TEGAR](https://ejournal.upi.edu/index.php/tegar/article/view/91162)). What is distinctly futsal-flavored is the *richness* layered onto each shot event. Lapresa et al. coded every shot by Spain's title-winning run at UEFA Futsal EURO 2010 across three linked fields — court zone, shot type/technique, and contact surface — and found statistically significant associations between zone and shot type (χ² = 55.821, p = 0.006) and between contact surface and shot type (χ² = 30.293, p = 0.017), using temporal pattern (T-pattern) analysis to recover tactical structure from the three-field combination ([Lapresa et al. 2013](https://investigacion.unirioja.es/documentos/5bbc69a3b750603269e81eca?lang=en)). A 2,449-goal study of the Spanish professional league similarly cross-tabulated shot zone, contact surface, shot type, method of play, and number of passes/players involved, finding most goals came from fast plays built on just one or two passes ([Zaguan 70195](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/70195/files/texto_completo.pdf?version=1)). Crucially, no study uses the same zone grid — samples range from six to nine court zones — and researchers explicitly warn against generalizing zone-specific percentages across age groups or competitions ([ubibliorum 15060](https://ubibliorum.ubi.pt/handle/10400.6/15060)), so a data model has latitude to define its own grid as long as it is applied consistently. Separately, a 56-match Brazilian study found teams with better final standings had statistically more goals *and* more "shots defended" (goalkeeper saves) with large effect sizes, meaning shot volume conceded functions as a team-level pressure indicator, not only an individual goalkeeper stat ([DOAJ 49d0c98733634dfa8e95daec48b45f3b](https://doaj.org/article/49d0c98733634dfa8e95daec48b45f3b)).

| Indicator | What it measures | Tracking granularity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | Ball fully crosses the line | Tally | Generic |
| Shot on target | Attempt that would score without intervention | Tally, often with outcome (saved/scored) | Generic |
| Shot off target | Attempt missing the goal frame | Tally | Generic |
| Shot blocked/intercepted | Attempt stopped by a defender before reaching the keeper | Tally, sometimes split "blocked" vs "intercepted" | Generic |
| Shot zone | Court sector the shot was taken from | Detail field (6–9 zones, study-specific) | Generic method, futsal-specific grid conventions |
| Shot type/technique | e.g., finta, chip (cavadinha), instep, toe-poke | Detail field, cross-tabulated with zone/surface | Generic concept; futsal-specific technique vocabulary |
| Contact surface | Which part of the foot/body struck the ball | Detail field | Generic |
| Assist | Pass immediately preceding a goal | Tally | Generic |
| Shooting accuracy / efficiency | Shots on target ÷ total shots; goals ÷ shots | Derived metric from tallies | Generic |

## Possession, passing, and phase-of-game indicators hinge on a four-part transition model unique in its naming

Portuguese-language methodology converges on **four moments of the game** — offensive organization, defensive organization, attack-to-defense transition, and defense-to-attack transition — explicitly treated as a coding convenience rather than a claim that phases are truly separable in play ([EFDeportes momentos](https://efdeportes.com/efd216/proposta-de-momentos-do-jogo-no-futsal.htm)). Within offensive organization, the same source splits plays into direct play, indirect play, individual actions, rehearsed collective plays, and group combinations, while defense-to-attack transition (the counter-attack) splits formally into **direct** (long balls or fast dribbling to catch a disorganized defense) and **indirect** (quick passing to build numerical superiority before finishing). A parallel research tradition instead uses three offensive methods — positional attack, fast attack, and counter-attack — treating "fast attack" as an intermediate category, though this three-way split is less consensual across research groups ([Conexões/Unicamp](https://periodicos.sbu.unicamp.br/ojs/index.php/conexoes/article/download/8637592/pdf/7640)). Either way, "attack origin" itself is the most consistently used top-level categorical field across independent Brazilian and Spanish studies, typically coded as one of four values — positional attack, counter-attack, set piece, or goleiro-linha play — even though their relative shares swing by competition: one dataset split them almost evenly (24.3% / 24.3% / 23.1% / 21.8%) ([Dialnet 4901928](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/4901928.pdf)), a 2017 Liga Nacional de Futsal sample put set pieces first at 31% ahead of counter-attack (21%) and positional attack (19%) ([UFSC 187758](https://repositorio.ufsc.br/handle/123456789/187758)), and yet another sample found counter-attack dominant at 37.25% ahead of positional attack's 30.85% ([Dialnet 4901919](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/4901919.pdf)). A dedicated 1,043-counter-attack study of the Brazilian league (2019/20) found direct ball recovery was the dominant trigger (86.6%), with only 40.7% of counter-attacks even reaching a shot and 6.0% ending in a goal ([UFG 142](https://revistas.ufg.br/fef/article/download/142/1523?inline=1)); Santana (2004) names four typical counter-attack triggers — pass interception, tackle (desarme), goalkeeper save, or a quick set-piece restart — that function as a valuable sub-indicator qualifying *why* a transition started, not just that one occurred ([Dialnet 6681224](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/6681224.pdf)). Defensive organization pairs with this via a **four-lines-of-marking** scheme (Line 1: high pressure on opponent's build-out; Line 2: still in the attacking half but not pressing the build-out; Line 3: marking at the defensive half-court; Line 4: deep marking in front of the goal area) that functions as futsal's rough equivalent to "high press / mid-block / low block" vocabulary from 11-a-side football, which did not appear as a standardized label in the futsal sources reviewed ([EFDeportes momentos](https://efdeportes.com/efd216/proposta-de-momentos-do-jogo-no-futsal.htm)).

| Indicator | What it measures | Tracking granularity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attack type (positional / counter-attack / set piece / goleiro-linha) | Top-level origin of a possession or goal | Tally with detail (4-value categorical) | Goleiro-linha value is futsal-specific; the other three are generic |
| Counter-attack sub-type (direct vs. indirect) | Long-ball/dribble finish vs. quick-passing numerical buildup | Detail field on counter-attack events | Generic concept; this exact binary naming is Brazilian-futsal-specific |
| Counter-attack trigger | Interception, tackle, GK save, or quick restart that started it | Detail field | Generic |
| Pass completion | Correct vs. incorrect pass | Tally with outcome | Generic |
| Ball possession | Time/share of play with the ball | Tally (timed) | Generic |
| Ball loss | Possession surrendered in open play | Tally, often with detail (zone, cause) | Generic |
| Defensive marking line (1–4) | Which of four pressing bands a defensive action occurred in | Detail field on defensive events | Futsal-specific naming convention |
| Defensive system (individual / zone / mixed) | Team's marking scheme | Contextual/match-level tag | Generic |

## Defensive actions and numerical-situation play are the least standardized layer of the taxonomy

Baseline defensive indicators — tackles/desarmes, interceptions, steals (roubadas de bola), and blocks — recur across studies and were explicitly flagged by two independent reviews (a 2016 systematic review of 79 studies and a 2024 AFC Women's Championship study) as *under-researched relative to offensive/shot indicators*, meaning any defensive taxonomy in a product will be drawing on thinner, less standardized academic ground than the shooting taxonomy above ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rpanxx/v16y2016i2p652-686.html); [UPI/TEGAR](https://ejournal.upi.edu/index.php/tegar/article/view/91162)). Three marking systems are named consistently in Brazilian coaching/academic sources — individual marking (each defender tracks a specific opponent across the court, described as the most common base system), zone marking (positioning referenced to the ball, now considered a situational tool rather than a permanent system at elite level), and mixed marking (a hybrid, e.g., individually marking the opponent who "unbalances" the game while others hold zone) ([EFDeportes defesa mista](https://efdeportes.com/efd174/treinamento-da-defesa-mista-no-futsal.htm)). "Counter-pressing" (pressing immediately after losing the ball) appears almost exclusively under its football-imported English/German name in FIFA Training Centre material, with only one explicit futsal drill ("Pressing 4v4 plus 4") located and a target recovery window of roughly 5–7 seconds before the team is coached to drop and stay compact ([FIFA Training Centre counter-pressing](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/practice/elite-sessions/transition-to-defending/counter-pressing.php)) — meaning futsal-native pressing vocabulary is thin and a product may need to borrow terms rather than cite an established futsal instrument. Numerical-situation indicators split sharply by cause: the only dedicated peer-reviewed study (Gómez et al., 2019, Journal of Sports Sciences) analyzed 125 attacks following an opponent's dismissal, finding attacking efficiency rose when the attacking team played at home, in minutes 33–36, and when the defending team already had 3 fouls accumulated, with four cluster-derived "types" of superiority attack identified but not named in the retrieved abstract ([UCM docta](https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/855367e2-2cbc-4bc0-84fa-b1431f020acf)). By contrast, essentially all other numerical-situation research folds "4v5 / 5v4" directly into the goleiro-linha context rather than treating red-card-driven inferiority as its own tracked category — a genuine, confirmed gap in the literature across two independent search rounds — so a data model is better served by one generic "numerical situation" indicator carrying a **cause field** (goleiro-linha, red card, other) than by building separate schemas per cause.

| Indicator | What it measures | Tracking granularity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tackle/desarme | Dispossessing an opponent via a direct challenge | Tally | Generic |
| Interception | Reading and cutting out a pass | Tally | Generic |
| Steal (roubada de bola) | Taking the ball without a direct tackle | Tally | Generic |
| Block | Defensive action stopping a shot/pass with the body | Tally | Generic |
| Defensive system tag | Individual / zone / mixed marking in use | Match or sequence-level tag | Generic |
| Numerical situation | Team playing 4v5 or 5v4 | Tally with detail (cause: goleiro-linha vs. red card vs. other; duration) | Generic concept; goleiro-linha cause is futsal-specific |
| Pressing/counter-press event | Immediate press after losing possession | Tally, sometimes with outcome (regain within window) | Generic |

## Set pieces and discipline share a hybrid indicator that has no football equivalent

Corner, throw-in, free kick, and penalty are themselves generic categories, and a 2024 Liga Nacional de Futsal sample of 346 set-piece goals split them into corner kicks (~32%), free kicks (~24%), and throw-ins (~23%) ([static.even3.com 1337492](https://static.even3.com/anais/1337492.pdf)), while a small single-team case study (8 matches, 95 throw-ins and 43 corner-kick actions coded) found only corners produced goals in that sample ([revistas.unipar.br 3415](https://revistas.unipar.br/index.php/saude/article/download/3415/2317/11102)). What is categorically futsal-specific is the **accumulated-team-foul rule**: per FIFA's official explainer (corroborated by Argentina's 2025–26 AFA rules bulletin), once a team commits its 6th foul of a period, the opponent is awarded a direct free kick **with no defensive wall**, taken from the second penalty spot 10 m from goal — colloquially a "double penalty" — and this threshold is disputed by at least one Spanish-language secondary source claiming the 5th foul, a discrepancy the primary FIFA/AFA sources do not support and that should be verified against CBFS's official text before being hard-coded ([FIFA Inside](https://inside.fifa.com/news/the-rules-of-futsal-explained); [AFA bulletin 6758-01](https://assets1.afa.com.ar/media/MARU/2025/09-SEPTIEMBRE/Boletin-Complementario-6758-01-Reglas-de-Juego-del-Futsal-2025-26---Resumen-de-los-Principales-Cambios.pdf)). Only fouls sanctioned as direct free kicks or penalties count toward this total, regardless of whether advantage was played, and the count carries over into extra time within the same half ([FIFGS rules](https://www.rsf-id.ru/downloads/dokumenti/sportivnie_dokumenti/futsal-for-athletes-with-down-syndrome/fifgs-futsal-rules.pdf); [The FA Law 13](https://www.thefa.com/football-rules-governance/lawsandrules/laws/futsal/law-13---free-kicks)). This makes the accumulated-foul counter a hybrid indicator that is simultaneously a discipline metric (fouls per team per period) and an offensive set-piece trigger (it unlocks the no-wall free kick), and no source found treats it as a formal analytical KPI correlated with outcomes — it exists on the match sheet as a rule-mandated count, not yet as a studied performance variable, which is itself a finding about where the research has *not* gone. Beyond that, discipline tracking (yellow/red cards, fouls committed/suffered) is entirely generic; the one specific wrinkle is that red-card-driven numerical inferiority in open play, as noted above, has no dedicated tactical-adjustment literature in futsal specifically, unlike the heavily studied goleiro-linha numerical-advantage case.

| Indicator | What it measures | Tracking granularity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corner kick | Restart after ball exits over the goal line off a defender | Tally, with outcome (shot/goal resulting) | Generic |
| Throw-in | Restart after ball exits over the touchline | Tally, with outcome | Generic |
| Free kick (with wall) | Direct/indirect free kick under the normal accumulated-foul threshold | Tally, with outcome | Generic |
| Free kick without wall / "double penalty" | Direct free kick from the second penalty spot after the 6th+ team foul in a period | Tally with detail (team foul count at time of award) | Futsal-specific |
| Penalty | Direct free kick from the first penalty spot (6 m) | Tally, with outcome | Generic |
| Accumulated team fouls (per period) | Running count of fouls that count toward the no-wall threshold | Running tally, period-scoped | Futsal-specific rule, generic foul concept |
| Yellow/red card | Disciplinary sanction | Tally | Generic |
| Rehearsed set-piece play (jogada ensaiada) | Pre-trained pattern for a specific restart | Tally with detail (play name/pattern) | Generic concept; prominent in futsal coaching vocabulary |

## Goalkeeper indicators split into shot-stopping and the goleiro-linha/linha-goleiro tactic, tracked as genuinely separate schemas

Shot-stopping research converges on **shot context** as the dominant explanatory variable rather than isolated goalkeeper attributes: a 260-match, roughly 4,400-shot multilevel study found an average save percentage of 84.3% ± 10.0%, with interventions **outside the penalty area** cutting the odds of a goal roughly two-thirds (OR = 0.33, p < 0.001), and goal probability further linked to shot distance and height ([citedrive determinants study](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/determinants-of-goalkeeper-defensive-performance-in-professional-futsal-a-multilevel-analysis-of-match-and-shot-contexts/)). Save **technique** is a recurring detail field: a men's futsal study found "save without falling" was the most common technique (37.41% of defensive actions) ahead of "falling using the feet" (15.11%) across 170 shots on target and 31 goals conceded ([Dialnet 6112202](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/6112202.pdf)), while a women's state-level study of 698 saves split them hands (66.48%), feet (10.74%), and goal-exit/claim (22.78%) ([DOAJ e5f3765926b847df87e150d82b46c71b](https://doaj.org/article/e5f3765926b847df87e150d82b46c71b)) — figures that should be treated as provisional since full texts could not be independently verified. No source documents a standardized "1-on-1 won/lost" statistic; it remains a coaching concept rather than a published metric. Distribution is qualitatively framed as the goalkeeper's "first pass" by FIFA and coaching sources, but the only quantitative study found — Indonesia's 2018 Professional Futsal League Final Four — measured goalkeeper throw distribution generating a successful counter-attack just **3.85%** of the time ([repository.upi.edu 48514](https://repository.upi.edu/48514)), implying raw attempt counts may matter more than success rate for this rare, high-value event.

The flying-goalkeeper tactic is where futsal diverges most sharply from any 11-a-side analogue, and the clearest single finding in this research is that Brazilian academic literature tracks it as **two separately coded tactics**, not one: "goleiro-linha" (the goalkeeper advances to play as an auxiliary attacker) and "linha-goleiro" (an outfield player substitutes into the goalkeeper's role) ([Dialnet 6816971](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/6816971.pdf)). In a 10-game Liga Nacional de Futsal sample, goleiro-linha plays (n=183) were triggered mainly by throw-in restarts (44.83%) and opponent pressing (28.41%), while linha-goleiro plays (n=45) were triggered mainly by positional attack (42.22%), corners (28.89%), and throw-ins (26.67%); both tactics were tracked with the same outcome fields used for any outfield possession — goals, wrong passes, shots on/off target, balls lost, fouls for/against, penalties for/against — layered on top of GK-specific situational tags (triggering situation, ball-reception zone, scoreline). Shots were attempted in 43% of goleiro-linha plays and 22% of linha-goleiro plays, but conversion was extremely low (1.6% and 0% respectively), a pattern that repeats across every quantitative study found: FIFA's own World Cup trend data shows fly-goalkeeper deployments up 74.5% from 2021 to 2024 with goal conversion rising only from 1% to 5.9%, while the related "powerplay" (5v4 substitution) saw deployments up 38.1% even as its goal rate *fell* from 7.6% to 4.2% and its **concede rate stayed higher than its score rate** at 6.5% in 2024 ([FIFA Training Centre FFWC 2024](https://fifatrainingcentre.com/en/game/tournaments/ffwc/2024/understanding-fifa-futsal-world-cup-2024-in-5-key-observations.php)). A separate three-league European study (673 fly-goalkeeper goals) found the tactic accounted for 15.33% of all goals scored in that sample ([Zaguan 89783](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/89783)), and a related point-classification study found goals scored while defending conventionally converted to match points 97.9% of the time, versus only 35.1% when the goal was scored during a team's *own* flying-goalkeeper attack — treating "conceding during your own power play" as a classification-relevant risk indicator even without a dedicated term for it ([Zaguan 126201](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/126201)). No source used a Portuguese term equivalent to handball's "baliza deserta" (empty net) for this scenario; it exists as a risk pattern in the data, not as a named statistic. Finally, the tactic has a documented team-level cost beyond individual GK stats: one study found teams measurably reduced the size and the variability of their defensive shape specifically while the goalkeeper played out as an outfield player, meaning a complete goleiro-linha indicator set should pair individual possession-event stats with a team defensive-shape metric ([futsalfocus.net 6972](https://futsalfocus.net/?p=6972)).

| Indicator | What it measures | Tracking granularity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Save | Shot-stopping action preventing a goal | Tally with outcome | Generic |
| Save technique | Hands vs. feet; with/without falling | Detail field on save events | Generic concept, futsal-specific technique vocabulary |
| Save zone (shot origin / GK position in-or-out-of-box) | Where the shot came from and whether the keeper intervened inside/outside the box | Detail field | Generic |
| Goalkeeper distribution | Throw, roll, or kick to start possession | Tally with outcome (led to counter-attack or not) | Generic |
| Goleiro-linha play | Goalkeeper advances as a 5th attacker | Tally with detail (trigger, reception zone, scoreline, outcome: shot/goal/wrong pass/loss/foul) | Futsal-specific |
| Linha-goleiro play | Outfield player substitutes in as goalkeeper | Tally with detail (same outcome fields as goleiro-linha) | Futsal-specific |
| Powerplay (formal 5v4 substitution) | Deployment of the extra-attacker numeric overload | Tally with outcome (goal/concede/neither) | Futsal-specific |
| Defensive shape metric while GK plays out | Area and variability of defensive shape during goleiro-linha | Team-level derived metric, not a per-event tally | Futsal-specific |
| Goal conceded during own flying-GK attack | Team concedes into a vacated goal while running goleiro-linha | Tally, flagged against the possession event | Futsal-specific |

## Conclusion

The clearest signal across the research notes is that futsal's statistical identity is not in its shot or pass taxonomy — those are nearly interchangeable with any invasion sport's — but in the handful of indicators built directly from its rulebook and its unlimited-substitution structure: the accumulated-team-foul "double penalty," and the goleiro-linha/linha-goleiro/powerplay trio, all of which show the same qualitative fingerprint of high attempt volume and persistently low, often negative, conversion. For a data model, this argues for keeping the generic layer (shots, passes, tackles, interceptions, set pieces, cards) as simple, reusable tally-or-tally+outcome event types shared with any team-sport schema, while giving the futsal-specific layer its own richer event shape carrying situational context (trigger, zone, scoreline, team-foul count at the time) as first-class fields rather than bolt-on notes — because in every study that tracked them, it was precisely that context (not the raw count) that explained the outcome. The one area flagged repeatedly as genuinely under-built, in both academic and commercial sources, is defensive and pressing vocabulary: no futsal-native taxonomy for counter-pressing, red-card-driven numerical inferiority, or a validated shot/save zone grid was found, so a product building these fields will be setting a convention rather than following an established one, and should document that choice rather than present it as an industry standard.
